# Pip’s Little Garden — review concept

This is lesson 9 of the independent ages 4–6 concept review pack. It contains one generated story illustration and five deterministic question diagrams. Nothing is integrated into the game.

## Teaching intent

A child chooses a care action for an **already-growing seedling**, using a spoken soil condition and a demonstrated light comparison. The useful takeaway is: “A growing plant needs light and water. I check the soil before adding more water.”

Pip needs help caring for a small garden. We revisit it across pretend days, so plant growth is not presented as an instant reward for a tap. The introduction is: “These little plants are already growing! Let's help them get water and light. Growing takes time, so we'll visit again on pretend days.”

The entry activity identifies the tool that can water soil. The second round compares suitable light with a dark cupboard while holding watering constant. The pale cupboard plant is taller and thinner: size alone is not a sign of healthy growth. The third round checks the “more is always better” water misconception. The fourth changes the pot and leaf shapes to test transfer. The fifth combines two needs as an optional stretch.

Children can point or select a large pictured choice. They do not need to read, drag accurately, respond quickly, or memorize a sequence. A future child prototype should speak the labels and allow replay.

## Questions and teaching detail

### 1. A drink for the seedling

**Pip asks:** “This seedling's soil is dry. Which thing can give it water?”

**Choices:** Watering can / Hat.

**Answer:** Watering can.

**Feedback:** “The watering can gives water to the dry soil. We add a little, then check the soil.”

**Three hints:**

1. Look at the soil. Pip tells us it is dry.
2. Imagine tipping each thing. Which one can pour water?
3. A hat does not pour water. The watering can does.

**Future visual explanation:** Show a measured small pour into the soil; the soil becomes damp. Keep the plant size unchanged in this moment.

**What this reveals:** Establishes the concrete water-care action before asking the child to diagnose a new plant.

### 2. Light makes a difference

**Pip asks:** “Both seedlings got water for several pretend days. Which place helped one grow sturdy green leaves?”

**Choices:** Dark cupboard / Lit garden spot.

**Answer:** Lit garden spot.

**Feedback:** “The garden spot gave this seedling suitable light. Its leaves stayed green and its stem grew sturdy.”

**Three hints:**

1. Look at the leaves and stems, not just how tall the plants are.
2. Compare the pale thin plant with the sturdy green one. Both got water.
3. The cupboard plant got too little light. This lit garden spot helped the other grow sturdy leaves.

**Future visual explanation:** Replay matched starting seedlings, equal suitable watering, and a day-night sequence. Reveal changes only across pretend days. Outline stems and leaves after the child asks for a hint.

**What this reveals:** Uses an explained comparison to establish light as a need; checks the tempting misconception that the tallest plant must be the healthiest.

### 3. Enough water for now

**Pip asks:** “We just watered until the soil was damp. Should we check it later, or pour more now?”

**Choices:** Check later / Pour more now.

**Answer:** Check later.

**Feedback:** “It has enough water for now. We check the soil later and water when it needs it.”

**Three hints:**

1. Remember: the soil is already damp.
2. Picture more and more water filling the pot. Plants do not need a puddle around their roots.
3. Choose checking later. Extra water now could make the soil too wet.

**Future visual explanation:** On request, compare damp soil with a flooded pot; do not turn the plant instantly brown. Future feedback should show checking moisture, rather than a fixed daily watering rule.

**What this reveals:** Prevents the lesson from becoming 'always add water' and introduces looking before acting.

### 4. New plant, same need

**Pip asks:** “This different plant already has light, but its soil is dry. What should we do?”

**Choices:** Cover it / Give a little water.

**Answer:** Give a little water.

**Feedback:** “The dry soil needs a little water. A different pot and different leaves do not change that need.”

**Three hints:**

1. What does this plant already have? Pip says it has light.
2. Look at the dry soil. Would a dark lid bring water?
3. Give the soil a little water, then check that it is damp.

**Future visual explanation:** Keep the changed container and leaf shape throughout feedback. Show a small pour reaching the soil and stop when it is damp; no immediate growth.

**What this reveals:** Tests transfer across plant and container appearance rather than repetition of the first image.

### 5. Two needs together — optional stretch

**Pip asks:** “This seedling is in a dark cupboard and its soil is dry. Which plan gives it both things it needs?”

**Choices:** Light only / Water in cupboard / Light and water.

**Answer:** Light and water.

**Feedback:** “This plan gives suitable light and water. We keep checking it as pretend days pass.”

**Three hints:**

1. Pip noticed two things: a dark place and dry soil.
2. Look for a plan with a lit place and a watering can together.
3. Light alone leaves the soil dry. Water alone leaves it too dark. Choose suitable light and a little water.

**Future visual explanation:** Demonstrate each plan on a duplicate of the same plant. Pause after one need is addressed to name the remaining need; then run the complete plan over pretend days.

**What this reveals:** Optional combination challenge; all action plans are single choices, with no hidden multi-select.

## Finale and household follow-up

Pip checks the garden over several pretend days. The cared-for seedlings show new leaves. Pip says, 'We gave them what they needed, and we gave them time.'

With a grown-up, inspect an existing non-toxic potted plant. Gently feel the potting mix and ask whether it needs water; the grown-up follows the plant's own care guidance. Draw or photograph it today and again after a few days.

## Decisions for review

1. Does the dark-cupboard comparison make the light need clear without teaching that the tallest plant is best?
2. Is the 'check later' round understandable, or should a future prototype first demonstrate damp versus soaking-wet soil?
3. Should the two-needs final round be optional for every child, or offered only after the single-need rounds?

## Scientific and design limits

- Review concept only: no game integration, child testing, voice generation, or claims of validated learning outcomes.
- The lesson starts with living leafy seedlings. It makes no claim that every seed requires light to germinate.
- Water and light are selected learning needs, not a complete plant-care recipe. Species, temperature, nutrients, oxygen, drainage, and watering needs also matter.
- The lit garden is a deliberately suitable environment for this illustrative seedling; any window or any amount of sunlight is not automatically suitable.
- Dryness is stated in the spoken prompt. Soil color and leaf posture alone are not reliable real-world diagnoses.
- Growth and low-light changes happen across pretend days. Small pours change soil moisture, not plant size instantly.
- All pictured containers have drainage holes. No universal water amount or watering schedule is prescribed.
- Generated art sets the story; the five SVG scenes are the exact question references. The art does not establish species identity or biological timing.

## Sources checked

- [University of Minnesota Extension: Lighting for indoor plants and starting seeds](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/yard-and-garden/gardening-in-minnesota/lighting-for-indoor-plants). Supports the suitable-light comparison, pale or thin growth under low light, and the warning that excessive light also has risks.
- [University of Maryland Extension: Care of Vegetable Seedlings](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/care-vegetable-seedlings). Supports sufficient light for seedlings, moist rather than saturated growing medium, and avoiding the claim that an ordinary window is always enough.

These sources support the botanical simplification. They do not certify the five-round experience or establish that a 4–6-year-old can interpret these drawings independently. That remains a playtest question.
