# Pip’s next adventures — visual lesson review

Ten independent lesson concepts for ages 4–6. Each lesson has one generated story illustration, five exact question diagrams, three-stage hints, and three questions for adult review. These are proposals; no game integration or child playtesting is claimed.

## 1. Pip’s Rocket Lab

**Learning goal:** Predict the direction a balloon rocket or a rocket starts moving when gas escapes from its opening.

**A child could say or show:** Gas goes one way. The rocket gets pushed the other way.

![Smiling silver-gray Pip watches a blue balloon taped to a straw on a string between two supports. Soft puffs leave the balloon’s right-hand opening. An inactive silver-and-blue rocket stands nearby in daylight.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** Let’s try my balloon rocket! Air is a kind of gas. Watch: air goes out one way, and the balloon gets pushed the other way. Which way will it go next?

**Story:** Pip is trying a balloon on a string before imagining a space journey. Help Pip predict each launch, then use the same idea for a rocket.

### Question 1: Which way will the balloon go?

Air comes out toward the yellow triangle. Which marker will the balloon move toward?

**Choices:** Blue circle · Yellow triangle

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Blue circle. Air leaves toward the triangle. That pushes the balloon the other way, toward the circle.

**Hints:** Find the opening where the air comes out. → Watch a slow demonstration: the air leaves on one side while the balloon slides the other way. → The air goes toward the yellow triangle, so the balloon goes toward the blue circle.

**What it reveals:** An easy first prediction follows the introductory demonstration and checks the opposite-direction relationship.

### Question 2: Turn the balloon around

Pip turned the balloon around. Air comes out toward the blue circle. Which marker will the balloon move toward?

**Choices:** Blue circle · Yellow triangle

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Yellow triangle. Turning the opening around changes the push. Air goes toward the circle, and the balloon moves toward the triangle.

**Hints:** Look again at the opening. It changed sides. → Pretend one hand is escaping air. Move your other hand the opposite way. → Air goes toward the blue circle. The balloon goes the other way, toward the yellow triangle.

**What it reveals:** The changed example reveals whether the child uses the opening rather than repeating the last marker or following balloon color.

### Question 3: A balloon tied shut

This balloon is tied shut and held still. No air can escape. When Pip lets go, will it zoom along the string or stay here?

**Choices:** Zoom along the string · Stay here

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/round-3.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Stay here. No air is escaping, so there is no rocket push. On our level string, the still balloon stays here.

**Hints:** Find the knot. Is there an open place for the air to leave? → Compare it with the open balloon: the open one lets air out, but this one keeps air inside. → A tied balloon has no escaping air to give it a rocket push. This one stays where Pip left it.

**What it reveals:** Checks whether inflation alone is mistaken for propulsion. The question states no escaping air and no starting motion.

### Question 4: A rocket uses the same idea

This rocket starts still. Hot gas shoots down from its engine. Which way will the rocket start moving?

**Choices:** Up toward the star · Down toward the flower

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/round-4.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Up toward the star. The rocket pushes hot gas down, and the gas pushes the rocket up. It is the same opposite-direction idea as the balloon.

**Hints:** Look at the engine opening. Which way is the gas going? → Use the balloon idea: gas goes one way, and the rocket gets pushed the other way. → Gas goes down, so this rocket gets pushed up toward the star.

**What it reveals:** Transfers the mechanism from cold air in a balloon to hot gas in a rocket, while changing the direction to vertical.

### Question 5: Optional: the push works in space

This rocket is still in space. It sends gas toward the blue circle. Which way can it start moving?

**Choices:** It cannot move in space · Toward the blue circle · Toward the yellow triangle

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/rockets/round-5.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Toward the yellow triangle. Gas goes toward the circle, and the rocket gets pushed toward the triangle. Its own escaping gas gives the push, even in space.

**Hints:** The rocket brings its own gas-making materials. It can push gas out here too. → Imagine the balloon turned this way. Where would it get pushed? → The gas goes toward the blue circle. The rocket starts moving the opposite way, toward the yellow triangle.

**What it reveals:** An optional stretch checks opposite-direction transfer without a ground or nearby air. This introduces space thrust rather than testing prior space knowledge.

### Questions for your review

- Do the blue-circle and yellow-triangle markers make direction easier to point at, or would two familiar destination objects be clearer?
- Should the tied-balloon comparison remain round three, or would a second open-balloon prediction better fit the Gems lesson’s existing challenge level?
- Should the space example stay as the optional fifth round, or be saved for a later rocket lesson after children have tried the physical balloon?

**Away from the screen:** With a grown-up, thread a string through a straw and stretch the string level between two supports. The adult inflates a balloon, holds its neck closed, and tapes it to the straw. Point to where its air will escape, predict its travel, and release. Reverse the balloon and try again. The adult handles balloons and cleans up any broken pieces.

## 2. Pip’s Rolling Garage

**Learning goal:** Choose wheels with a round outer edge for a smoother ride on a smooth ramp, even when their size, decoration, or vehicle changes.

**A child could say or show:** Round wheels have no corners, so they can give a smooth ride on our smooth ramp.

![Pip, a small silver-gray smiling ball with blue seams, stands beside a blue toy delivery car on a gentle ramp. A tray holds round and square spare wheels, and a parcel waits at an open blue garage.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** My parcel needs a ride! Let’s choose some wheels for our smooth ramp.

**Story:** Pip needs to deliver a parcel down a gentle smooth ramp. The toy car has its wheels off, and the child helps choose replacements before trying a parcel wagon.

### Question 1: Repair the car

Which wheels would give my car a smoother ride on this smooth ramp?

**Choices:** Square wheels · Round wheels

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Round wheels. These wheels have a round edge. They can turn smoothly on our smooth ramp.

**Hints:** Look at the outside edge of each wheel. → Let’s turn one wheel from each set slowly on our smooth ramp. → The square’s corners lift the car up and down. The round edge gives the car a smoother ride.

**What it reveals:** Connects round wheel geometry to a concrete job, rather than asking only for a shape name.

### Question 2: Look past the picture

Which wheels have a round outside edge, even with a star picture inside?

**Choices:** Wheels with star pictures · Plain square wheels

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Wheels with star pictures. The star is a picture inside. The outside edge is still round, so these wheels can turn smoothly here.

**Hints:** The picture is inside. Follow the outside edge. → Let’s trace around each wheel, without tracing its picture. → The star picture does not change the wheel’s round outside edge.

**What it reveals:** Changes appearance while preserving the useful property. Avoids teaching children to choose only plain or familiar wheels.

### Question 3: Small and big

Which set has wheels with no corners? More than one set can work.

**Choices:** Small round wheels · Square wheels · Big round wheels

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/round-3.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Small round wheels or Big round wheels. Yes, those wheels have a round edge. Small and big wheels can both be round.

**Hints:** Look for an outside edge with no corners. → Let’s trace the small wheels and then the big wheels. → Both round sets have no corners. You can choose either one.

**What it reveals:** Checks whether size distracts from roundness. Does not compare speeds, distances, or claim all sizes work equally well on every car.

### Question 4: A new parcel wagon

This wagon needs a smoother ride on the same ramp. Which wheels would you choose?

**Choices:** Square wheels · Round wheels

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/round-4.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Round wheels. The wagon can use round wheels too. Their round edge helps give a smoother ride on our smooth ramp.

**Hints:** The vehicle changed. Look at the wheels’ outside edges. → Let’s turn the wheels slowly under the wagon, just as we did under the car. → Round wheels have no corners lifting this wagon up and down on our smooth ramp.

**What it reveals:** Transfers the idea to a different vehicle while holding the ramp and parcel task constant.

### Question 5: Closer look — optional stretch

These wheels look almost alike. Which set has a round edge with no corners?

**Choices:** Round-edge wheels · Many-cornered wheels

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/cars/round-5.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Round-edge wheels. This edge curves all the way around. The other wheels have little corners. The round edge gives a smoother ride on our smooth ramp.

**Hints:** Look slowly around the outside edge. → Let’s trace each edge. One keeps curving; one has short straight pieces. → Choose the edge that curves all the way around without corners.

**What it reveals:** An optional close comparison reveals whether the child recognizes continuous roundness rather than any roughly wheel-like silhouette.

### Questions for your review

- Does the smoother-ride goal feel more useful and understandable than a fastest-car question?
- Should round three accept either round wheel set, or should the first version use only two choices for easier visual scanning?
- Is the circle-versus-eight-sided stretch worth keeping as an optional fifth round, or should that round instead invite a free wheel experiment?

**Away from the screen:** With a grown-up, slowly turn a large round cardboard disk and a same-width square cardboard piece along a smooth tabletop. Keep fingers at the center and notice how that center moves. Then release an existing toy car from the same low book-ramp spot twice. Observe rather than race; the adult cuts the cardboard and holds the ramp.

## 3. Track Rescue

**Learning goal:** Choose or identify connected track by checking that the rails join from the train to the station.

**A child could say or show:** The train needs joined track all the way.

![Pip, a silver-gray ball with blue seams and a smiling face, sits beside a straight and curved wooden toy track piece. A blue locomotive and yellow carriage wait at a visible gap, with a blue-roofed station beyond.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** I'm Pip! My toy train needs to get to the station. Can you help me join its track?

**Story:** Pip has a package on a toy train, but its railway has a missing piece. Help repair the track and choose a route that reaches the station.

### Question 1: A straight repair

Which piece joins the track: the straight piece or the curved piece?

**Choices:** Straight piece · Curved piece

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Straight piece. The straight piece joins both ends. Now there is track all the way to the station.

**Hints:** Look at the two ends of the gap. → Imagine sliding each piece between the ends. Does it touch both? → Both ends point across. The straight piece connects them.

**What it reveals:** An easy start checks whether the child can match a single piece to two visible rail ends.

### Question 2: A bend to the station

This track needs to turn. Which piece joins the ends?

**Choices:** Straight piece · Curved piece

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Curved piece. The curved piece turns the track and joins both ends. The train can follow the bend to the station.

**Hints:** The train needs to go across, then up. → Follow the direction of each rail end with your finger. → The curve starts across and turns upward. Its two ends join the waiting rails.

**What it reveals:** A changed example checks that the child attends to the direction of both ends rather than always choosing straight.

### Question 3: Connected beats short

Which route can take the train all the way to the station: the upper route or the lower route?

**Choices:** Upper route · Lower route

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/round-3.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Lower route. The lower route is joined all the way. The upper route has a gap, so its train must stop there.

**Hints:** Check each route all the way from its train. → Trace the upper route, then the lower route. Does your finger meet a gap? → The short route is broken. The longer route stays joined, so that train reaches its station.

**What it reveals:** A misconception check reveals whether the child chooses a shorter or straighter route despite a visible break.

### Question 4: Find the hidden stop

Where is the break: the left circle or the right circle?

**Choices:** Left circle · Right circle

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/round-4.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Left circle. The left circle has the gap. A piece of track needs to go there before the train can reach the station.

**Hints:** Start at the train and look along the rails. → Stop tracing when the rails stop. → The rails stop inside the left circle. The right circle already has joined rails.

**What it reveals:** Transfer: the child diagnoses a new route instead of choosing a supplied repair shape. Spoken left/right may be supported with gestures.

### Question 5: Two-piece rescue · optional stretch

Which pair joins this gap: two curves, two straights, or a straight and a curve?

**Choices:** Two curves · Two straights · Straight + curve

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/trains/round-5.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Straight + curve. The straight piece reaches across. The curved piece turns down and joins the station track. Together they fill the gap.

**Hints:** The train needs to go across, then turn down. → Try a pair in your mind. Is there a piece to reach across and one to make the turn? → Put the straight piece first. Then the curve turns down to meet the station track.

**What it reveals:** An optional stretch combines two previously shown shapes in one prediction. Skip freely when the child is ready to finish.

### Questions for your review

- Are single-piece repairs followed by route comparison the right difficulty progression, or should the broken-route comparison come earlier?
- Should round four use two spoken position choices, or should a future version let the child tap the break anywhere along the track?
- Does the two-piece fifth round feel like a useful optional stretch, or should the finale arrive after four rounds for most children?

**Away from the screen:** With a grown-up, lay strips of paper on the floor as a toy railway. Leave one visible gap. Ask the child to join it, then move a toy along the whole path. Make a bend and try again. A finger can stand in for a train.

## 4. Pip’s Alphabet Post Office

**Learning goal:** Recognize a named uppercase M across size, color, position, and simple print variations; distinguish it from a different upright letter.

**A child could say or show:** I can find M even when it looks a little different.

![Silver-gray spherical Pip with blue curved seams beside a wooden sorting table and envelopes; three blue, teal, and yellow mailboxes have completely blank panels in a bright outdoor scene.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** Welcome to my post office! Today we’re looking for one letter. This is M. Can you say M with me? Help me find M on the mailboxes.

**Story:** Pip has envelopes to deliver. Each mailbox has a letter, and the child helps find the matching letter so the mail reaches its home.

### Question 1: Meet M

This envelope shows M. Which mailbox also shows M?

**Choices:** M — left mailbox · S — right mailbox

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** M — left mailbox. The envelope and this mailbox both show M. The letter matches, so the envelope can go here.

**Hints:** Look at the letter on the envelope, then look at each mailbox. → Watch Pip trace the tall sides and the middle lines of M. Look for those lines on a mailbox. → This mailbox has M, just like the envelope. Let’s deliver it together.

**What it reveals:** An easy visual match establishes one letter and the delivery action without assuming previous alphabet knowledge.

### Question 2: A new-looking M

This red letter is M. Which mailbox shows M, even in a different color?

**Choices:** B — left mailbox · S — middle mailbox · M — right mailbox

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** M — right mailbox. The green letter is also M. A letter can change color or size and keep its name.

**Hints:** Look at the lines of M. Its color does not tell us its name. → Show the red M changing to green and growing while its lines stay the same. Then return to the choices. → The large green M has the same letter shape as the little red M. That mailbox is the match.

**What it reveals:** Separates letter identity from color and size, with a same-color distractor and a changed answer location.

### Question 3: M and W

These letters look a little alike. Which mailbox shows M?

**Choices:** W — left mailbox · M — right mailbox

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/round-3.svg)

**Accepted answer:** M — right mailbox. This letter is M. The other letter is W. We look at the whole letter and the way it stands.

**Hints:** Start with the M on the envelope. Compare its outside lines with each mailbox letter. → Trace the two tall straight outside lines of the displayed M, then compare the displayed W’s slanting outside lines. → The right mailbox shows the same upright M as the envelope. The left mailbox shows W.

**What it reveals:** Checks a plausible visual confusion while keeping letter orientation meaningful.

### Question 4: Another printed M

This letter is M, with little feet on its lines. Which mailbox shows M too?

**Choices:** A — left mailbox · M — middle mailbox · N — right mailbox

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/round-4.svg)

**Accepted answer:** M — middle mailbox. Both letters are M. Some printed M letters have little feet, and some do not.

**Hints:** Look for M’s two tall sides and the lines meeting in the middle. → Place the serif M and a plain M side by side. Trace the main lines, then briefly show the small feet. → The middle mailbox shows M. Its main letter shape matches, even without the little feet.

**What it reveals:** Transfers recognition across a modest familiar print variation rather than exact pixel matching.

### Question 5: Optional: big M, little m

This is big M, and this is little m. They are both M. Which mailbox has little m?

**Choices:** n — left mailbox · w — middle mailbox · m — right mailbox

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/letters/round-5.svg)

**Accepted answer:** m — right mailbox. This is little m. Big M and little m are two ways to write the letter M.

**Hints:** Look at little m on our card. Find its shape again on a mailbox. → Trace little m’s two rounded humps on the card. Keep the card visible while we compare the choices. → The right mailbox has little m, like the card. Big M and little m can look different and share the name M.

**What it reveals:** Offers a supported uppercase/lowercase association only after a demonstration. Skippable; completion of the four recognition rounds is sufficient.

### Questions for your review

- Should the first play session focus on M for everyone, or let a grown-up choose a familiar letter from the child’s name? Personalization would need equivalent reviewed scenes.
- Does the M-versus-W comparison feel useful and clear, or should a first-play version keep a more visually distinct distractor and reserve W for repeat play?
- Should the optional M/m pairing appear only after the four uppercase rounds, or become a separate later lesson to keep the first session focused?

**Away from the screen:** With a grown-up, look for M on a cereal box, a book, or a handwritten card. Say its name and compare its appearance. Make a paper mailbox and deliver a homemade M envelope. Use familiar letters from the child’s name in a later session.

## 5. Pip’s Picnic Pack

**Learning goal:** Use a spoken everyday word or short functional description to choose a suitable pictured object, including a changed example.

**A child could say or show:** Things can look different and still help us do the same job.

![A smiling silver-gray ball with blue seams sits beside an open picnic basket, a blue handled cup, a yellow handleless cup, a plate and bread roll, a bowl of smooth soup, a spoon, a cloth napkin, and an apple on a teal blanket in a sunny park.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** Will you help me pack my picnic? Listen, then point to a thing that can help.

**Story:** Pip is packing a picnic and needs help finding objects for a drink, soup, and a small spill.

### Question 1: A drink for Pip

Pip is thirsty. Which thing helps Pip drink water?

**Choices:** Blue handled cup · Shallow plate

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Blue handled cup. The cup holds a drink. Pip can lift it and take a sip.

**Hints:** Look for a place to hold Pip’s drink. → Pretend to lift each thing and take a sip. Which is made to hold a drink? → This is a cup. Water stays inside its deep sides, ready for a sip.

**What it reveals:** Starts with a familiar functional association and two distinct silhouettes; no reading or naming aloud is required.

### Question 2: A cup can look different

Find a cup for Pip’s drink.

**Choices:** Shallow plate · Apple · Yellow handleless cup

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Yellow handleless cup. This cup has no handle, and it is still a cup. Its sides hold a drink.

**Hints:** A cup does not have to be blue. → Look at the open top and deep sides. Try imagining a drink inside. → The yellow one is a cup too. Cups can have different colors and shapes, with or without a handle.

**What it reveals:** Changes color, shape, handle, and position so a remembered blue-cup picture is insufficient.

### Question 3: Scoop smooth soup

Pip has smooth soup. Which tool can scoop it?

**Choices:** Fork · Spoon

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/round-3.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Spoon. The spoon’s little bowl can scoop the smooth soup. A fork’s gaps let it run through.

**Hints:** Think about lifting some smooth soup. → Look closely at the end of each tool. One has a little bowl; one has gaps. → The spoon can hold a scoop of smooth soup in its little bowl. Choose the spoon.

**What it reveals:** A closer comparison between two eating tools checks function rather than broad picnic membership.

### Question 4: Clean a little spill

Pip spilled water. Choose one thing that can wipe it up.

**Choices:** Sponge · Cloth napkin · Fork

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/round-4.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Sponge or Cloth napkin. A sponge or a cloth can wipe up this little spill. You found a thing that helps.

**Hints:** Look for something that can soak up a little water. → Imagine pressing the sponge or cloth onto the spill. They can take up water. → The sponge and the cloth can both help. Choose either one.

**What it reveals:** Transfers from naming containers and tools to an action word, and explicitly accepts two appropriate objects.

### Question 5: More than one way

Choose one thing that can hold water for drinking.

**Choices:** Blue handled cup · Fork · Deep bowl

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/words/round-5.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Blue handled cup or Deep bowl. The cup and the bowl can both hold water. People can drink from different kinds of vessels.

**Hints:** Look for an open inside with sides around it. → Imagine pouring a little water into the cup and the bowl. Where can it stay? → The cup and the bowl can both hold a drink. Choose either one.

**What it reveals:** Optional stretch separates the functional category from the word cup and welcomes familiar drinking practices from different households.

### Questions for your review

- Should the lesson mainly practice object names, or functional language such as drink, scoop, and wipe? The current version mixes both; which emphasis fits the Gems lesson’s scope?
- Do you want both sensible answers visible in rounds four and five, with any single choice accepted, or would a simpler first release use only one suitable option per question?
- Are these vessels and picnic objects familiar to the intended children, and which home-language words or household alternatives should the review include?

**Away from the screen:** With a grown-up, put out a cup, a bowl, a spoon, a fork, and a small cloth. Ask, 'What could help us have a drink?' and 'What could wipe up a little spill?' Accept a child’s sensible explanation; use a small amount of cool water only if wanted.

## 6. Shape Builders

**Learning goal:** Recognize a triangle by its three straight sides despite changes in orientation, size, width, or color.

**A child could say or show:** A triangle has three straight sides. Turning it or making it small keeps it a triangle.

![Smiling silver-gray Pip beside a small house with a yellow triangular roof; red triangle, blue circle, and green rectangular pieces lie on the ground.](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/shapes/concept.png)

**Pip’s introduction:** Let’s build my little house! I want a triangle roof. A triangle has three straight sides. We can look for them together.

**Story:** Pip is making a little picture house and needs a triangle for its roof. Different pieces look tempting; the child checks their sides to help.

### Question 1: Find our first triangle

A triangle has three straight sides. Which shape is a triangle?

**Choices:** Left shape · Right shape

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/shapes/round-1.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Right shape. This triangle has three straight sides, and they meet at three corners.

**Hints:** Look at the edges. Which shape has straight sides? → Let’s trace each straight side and tap each corner. → The right shape has three straight sides and three corners. The left shape has a curved edge.

**What it reveals:** An easy comparison introduces the defining property in the question rather than assuming prior shape vocabulary.

### Question 2: A triangle can turn

I turned the pieces. Which one is still a triangle?

**Choices:** Left shape · Right shape

[View the exact question scene](/Users/ewj/Desktop/projects/wwm/docs/education/lesson-concepts/2026-09-30/lessons/shapes/round-2.svg)

**Accepted answer:** Left shape. Turning this triangle does not change its three straight sides.

**Hints:** A triangle can point sideways. Check the sides. → Let’s follow the red shape all the way around and count its straight sides. → The red shape has three straight sides. The teal shape has four. The red shape is still a triangle.

**What it reveals:** Tests whether the child relies on an upright roof-like appearance instead of the defining property.

### Question 3: Small is still a triangle

Which piece is a triangle, even if it is little?
